K1 · Networking & Data Communication

Purposes, signals and transmission modes · KCSE 451 syllabus · KCSE 451 · Form 4 · Networking and data communication · lesson page

Reading cards

🌐 What a network is, and why we network Card 1

A computer network is a collection of independent computers and other devices linked together by a transmission medium so that they can communicate and share resources. Purposes of networking: resource sharing (data, programs and hardware such as one printer for a whole lab), remote communication (users far apart exchange messages and files, as when a bank branch in Kisumu updates records held in Nairobi), distributed processing (tasks and data are spread across several computers instead of one central machine), cost effectiveness (shared hardware and software licences cost less than buying for every user) and reliability (if one computer fails, data can be fetched from another and work continues). Networks are classified by area as LAN (local area network: one building or campus such as a school), MAN (metropolitan area network: a town or city) and WAN (wide area network: a country or the world, the internet being the largest).

⚠️ Limitations of networking Card 2

Networks also bring problems. Security threats: data is exposed to hackers, viruses and eavesdropping as it moves across the network. High initial cost: cables, devices, network software and trained staff are expensive to set up. Moral and cultural effects: easy access to the internet can expose users, especially the young, to pornography and other harmful material, and can erode cultural values. Over-reliance: when an organisation depends fully on its network, a breakdown stops all work, as when a bank's system goes down and no transactions can be made.

📶 Data communication terms Card 3

Data communication is the transmission of data from one location to another. A data signal is the electromagnetic or voltage form in which data travels. An analogue signal is a continuous wave that varies smoothly; a digital signal is discrete, made of on/off pulses (1s and 0s). Modulation converts digital data into analogue form for transmission and demodulation converts it back; a modem (modulator-demodulator) does both. Multiplexing sends several signals at the same time over one medium; a multiplexer combines them and a demultiplexer separates them at the other end. Bandwidth is the maximum amount of data a channel can carry per second, measured in bits per second (bps). Baseband carries one signal at a time using the whole bandwidth of the medium, as in most LANs; broadband carries many signals at once on different frequencies. Attenuation is the loss of signal strength as it travels further along a medium. Two other impairments spoil data on the way: noise, unwanted signals from outside (such as electrical interference) that mix with the data, and distortion, where the shape of the signal changes so the receiver may misread it. Guided (bounded) media direct signals along a physical path such as a cable; unguided (unbounded) media send signals through air or space.

↔️ Transmission modes, serial and parallel Card 4

Simplex: data flows in one direction only, for example radio and television broadcasts such as KBC (Kenya Broadcasting Corporation). Half duplex: data flows in both directions but only one way at a time, for example a walkie-talkie or police radio where users take turns. Full duplex: data flows in both directions at the same time, for example a telephone conversation. Serial transmission sends bits one after another along a single line; it is cheaper and reliable over long distances, so it is used in USB (Universal Serial Bus), network cables and telephone lines. Parallel transmission sends several bits at the same time along separate lines; it is faster but only suitable for short distances because bits can arrive out of step (skew), so it was used for old printer cables and inside the computer's buses.

Remember: a modem modulates (digital to analogue) and demodulates (analogue to digital); attenuation is loss of signal strength over distance; bandwidth is measured in bits per second; and always give an example with each transmission mode to earn the full marks.

K2 · Transmission Media

Bounded and unbounded media · KCSE 451 syllabus · KCSE 451 · Form 4 · Networking and data communication · lesson page

Reading cards

🔌 Twisted pair and coaxial cable Card 1

Twisted pair cable is made of pairs of insulated copper wires twisted around each other; the twisting reduces crosstalk (interference where a signal in one wire leaks into a neighbouring wire). Unshielded twisted pair (UTP) has no extra shielding and is the cheap, common LAN (Local Area Network) cable graded in categories such as Cat 5 (and Cat 5e) and Cat 6, the higher categories supporting faster speeds. A twisted pair network cable ends in an RJ-45 (Registered Jack 45) connector. Shielded twisted pair (STP) adds a foil or braided shield to protect against electromagnetic interference, but costs more. Twisted pair is cheap and easy to install, but suffers attenuation over long distances and has limited bandwidth. Coaxial cable has a central copper conductor, surrounded by an insulator (dielectric), a braided metal shield, and an outer plastic jacket. On networks it is fitted with a BNC (Bayonet Neill-Concelman) connector; on TV and satellite decoders it uses a screw-on F connector. Thin coaxial (thinnet) is flexible and used in LANs; thick coaxial (thicknet) is stiffer and carries signals further. Coaxial is more resistant to interference and has higher bandwidth than twisted pair, but is bulkier and more expensive; it is still used for TV aerials and satellite dishes.

💡 Fibre optic cable Card 2

Fibre optic cable carries data as pulses of light instead of electricity. Its parts are the core (very thin glass or plastic that carries the light), the cladding (surrounds the core and reflects light back into it), the buffer (a protective coating) and the jacket (the outer cover). Single-mode fibre has a very narrow core that carries one ray of light and is used over long distances; multimode fibre has a wider core that carries many rays and is used over shorter distances. Advantages: very high bandwidth, low attenuation so it carries data over long distances, immune to electromagnetic interference, very secure because it is hard to tap, and small and light. Disadvantages: expensive to buy and install, fragile, and difficult to join or repair, needing skilled technicians and special equipment. Kenya's undersea links such as SEACOM and TEAMS, which land at Mombasa, are fibre optic cables.

📡 Microwave and satellite Card 3

Microwave transmission sends high-frequency radio signals in a straight line between dish antennas, so the dishes must have a clear line of sight. Because the earth curves and buildings or hills block the signal, relay (repeater) stations are placed on hilltops and masts to receive, boost and pass the signal on. A communication satellite is a microwave relay station in space. Geostationary satellites orbit about 36,000 km above the equator and move with the earth's rotation, so they appear fixed above one spot. An earth station sends signals up to the satellite (uplink); the satellite's transponder receives, amplifies and changes the frequency of the signal, then sends it back down (downlink) to other earth stations over a very wide area. A VSAT (very small aperture terminal) is a small dish that lets a site such as a remote school, bank branch or petrol station connect by satellite. Satellites cover wide and remote areas, but are expensive to launch and maintain and the long distance causes a delay (latency).

📻 Radio, infrared, Bluetooth and cellular Card 4

Radio waves travel in all directions and do not need line of sight. They are grouped by frequency: high frequency (HF) carries far by bouncing off the ionosphere, very high frequency (VHF) is used for FM (Frequency Modulation) radio and two-way radios, and ultra high frequency (UHF) is used for television and mobile phones. Infrared uses invisible light over a very short distance with line of sight, as in a TV remote control. Bluetooth uses low-power radio waves to link devices within about 10 metres, such as a phone and a wireless headset. Cellular (mobile) networks, such as Safaricom's, divide an area into cells, each served by a base station (mast); a call or data session is handed from one cell to the next as the user moves. Wireless media allow mobility and reach places where cables are hard to lay, but they are less secure, can suffer interference from weather and other signals, and are often slower than fibre. When choosing a medium, consider cost, data transfer speed (bandwidth), the distance to be covered, ease of installation, resistance to interference (attenuation and noise) and security.

K3 · Network Devices, Topologies & Security

Devices, topologies and keeping networks safe · KCSE 451 syllabus · KCSE 451 · Form 4 · Networking and data communication · lesson page

Reading cards

🧰 Network devices Card 1

A network interface card (NIC) connects a computer to the network medium and gives it a unique physical (MAC) address. A modem converts digital data to analogue and back so computers can communicate over telephone lines. A hub connects computers in a star network and sends (broadcasts) every packet to all ports. A switch also connects computers, but it forwards each packet only to the port of the destination computer, which reduces traffic. A repeater receives a weak signal and regenerates (boosts) it so it can travel further, overcoming attenuation. A bridge joins two network segments (usually using the same protocol) and filters traffic between them. A router connects different networks and chooses the best path for each packet, such as linking a school LAN (Local Area Network) to the internet. A gateway connects networks that use different protocols by translating between them. A wireless access point (WAP) lets wireless devices join a wired network.

💽 Network software and network models Card 2

Networks need software as well as hardware. A network operating system (NOS), such as Windows Server or Linux, manages network resources, user accounts, security and access to shared files and printers. Protocols are the set of rules that govern how data is sent and received on a network; TCP/IP (Transmission Control Protocol/Internet Protocol) is the protocol used on the internet, where TCP breaks data into packets and reassembles them, and IP addresses and routes the packets. In a peer-to-peer network, all computers are equal and each user shares resources from their own computer; it is cheap and simple for a few computers but hard to secure and manage. In a client-server network, one or more powerful servers provide resources and services to client computers; it is centrally managed, more secure and easy to back up, but the server is costly and if it fails users lose access.

⚙️ Choosing a network operating system Card 3

When choosing a network operating system for a LAN (Local Area Network), consider: the number of users and computers it must support, and whether it can grow (scalability); its security features, such as user accounts, access rights and encryption; the cost of buying and licensing it; compatibility with the hardware and software already in use; ease of installation and administration; and the support and updates offered by its maker. Examples include Windows Server, Linux and UNIX.

🕸️ Topologies Card 4

Topology is the way computers are arranged in a network. Physical topology is the actual layout of devices and cables; logical topology is how data flows through the network. Star: each computer connects to a central hub or switch; a failed cable affects only one computer and it is easy to add devices, but if the central device fails the whole network fails and it uses a lot of cable. Bus: all computers share one backbone cable, each joined to it by a short drop cable through a T-connector, with a terminator at each end of the backbone to absorb signals so they do not bounce back; it is cheap and uses little cable, but a break in the backbone brings down the network and faults are hard to find. Ring: computers form a closed loop and data passes in one direction from one to the next; every computer gets equal access, but one failed computer or cable can break the whole ring. Mesh: each computer has links to many or all others; it is very reliable because data can take another route, but it is expensive and complex to cable. Tree (hierarchical): groups of star networks connected to a backbone in levels; it is easy to expand and manage, but if the backbone or a top-level device fails, the networks below it are cut off.

🔐 Network security Card 5

Share-level security protects each shared resource, such as a folder or printer, with its own password; anyone who knows the password can use it. User-level security gives each user an account with a username and password, and the administrator sets which resources each user can access; it is more secure and is used in client-server networks. A firewall is hardware or software that filters traffic between a private network and the internet, blocking unauthorised access. A proxy server sits between users and the internet, forwarding their requests; it can hide internal addresses, filter websites and cache (store) popular pages to speed up access. Encryption scrambles data into a coded form so that only someone with the right key can read it, protecting data as it travels. An audit trail is a record (log) of who accessed the system, when, and what they did, which helps to detect and investigate security breaches.

🎣 Threats and data integrity Card 6

Phishing tricks users, through fake emails or messages that look genuine, into giving away passwords, PINs (Personal Identification Numbers) or card details. Pharming redirects users from a genuine website to a fake one, for example by corrupting DNS (Domain Name System) records, so the victim lands on the fake site even after typing the correct address. Data integrity means data stays accurate, complete and consistent. Measures to protect it: validation and verification when data is entered; passwords and access rights so only authorised users can change data; regular backups kept off-site; antivirus software; audit trails; and a UPS (Uninterruptible Power Supply) so a power cut does not corrupt data being saved.

  • A hub sends data to all ports, but a switch sends it only to the right computer.
  • A router connects different networks; a gateway connects networks that use different protocols.

  • In a bus topology, a break in the backbone cable brings the network down.
  • Firewalls, proxy servers, encryption and audit trails help keep a network secure.

Servers centralise user accounts, security and backups; peer-to-peer is cheaper because every computer is equal, but it is harder to manage.

Q6. Logical topology describes...

  • The type and length of cables used
  • The physical position of computers in a room
  • ✓ How data flows through the network
  • The number of printers on the network

Physical topology is the actual layout of cables and devices; logical topology is the path data takes, which may differ from the physical layout.

Q7. What is a disadvantage of a star topology?

  • ✓ If the central hub or switch fails, the whole network fails
  • A break in any one computer's cable brings down the whole network
  • It is very hard to add new computers once it is set up
  • Data can travel only in one direction around a closed loop

All computers connect through the central device, so it is a single point of failure; a break in one computer's cable affects only that computer.

Q8. In a bus topology, what are terminators used for?

  • To connect the backbone to the internet through a router
  • To encrypt data before it travels along the backbone
  • To boost the signal so that it can reach computers at the far end of the backbone
  • ✓ To absorb signals at the backbone ends so they do not bounce back

Without terminators, signals reflect from the cable ends and collide with new data, so the network stops working properly.

Q9. A tree (hierarchical) topology is best described as...

  • Computers connected in a single closed loop, data passing from one to the next
  • ✓ Groups of star networks connected to a backbone in levels
  • Every computer linked directly to every other computer on the network
  • Two computers joined directly by one cable

A tree links several star networks in levels, which makes it easy to expand, as in a school with several labs joined to one backbone.

Q10. What does an audit trail provide?

  • A coded version of the data that only authorised users can read
  • A copy of popular web pages kept locally to speed up access
  • ✓ A record of who accessed the system, when, and what they did
  • A backup power supply that keeps the server running during outages

Audit trails log user activity so administrators can detect and investigate security breaches; encryption, caching and UPS are separate measures.

K4 · ICT in Business & Services

Banking, retail, booking, learning and communication · KCSE 451 syllabus · KCSE 451 · Form 4 · Application areas of ICT · lesson page

Reading cards

🏦 Financial systems Card 1

Businesses use accounting packages to record sales, purchases, invoices and ledgers, and payroll systems to calculate each worker's gross pay, deductions such as PAYE (Pay As You Earn) and NSSF (National Social Security Fund), and net pay, then print payslips. Banks use ICT (Information and Communication Technology) everywhere: automated teller machines (ATMs) let customers withdraw cash and check balances at any time using a card and PIN (Personal Identification Number); cheque clearing uses magnetic ink character recognition (MICR) to read the cheque number, bank code and account number printed in magnetic ink quickly and accurately (do not confuse it with OMR, optical mark recognition, which reads pencil marks on forms such as multiple-choice answer sheets); and internet and mobile banking let customers transfer money and pay bills without visiting a branch. M-Pesa is a form of electronic funds transfer (EFT): money moves from one account to another electronically, with no cash or cheque changing hands.

🛒 Retail systems Card 2

Supermarkets use point-of-sale (POS) terminals at the till. A barcode reader scans the product's barcode, and the computer looks up the item's name and price in the stock file, prints an itemised receipt and reduces the stock level. This gives automatic stock control: when an item falls below its reorder level, the system flags it or orders more from the supplier. Customers can pay by electronic funds transfer at point of sale (EFTPOS), using a debit card or mobile money, so the amount is moved straight from the customer's account to the shop's account.

✈️ Reservation systems Card 3

Reservation systems let customers book seats or rooms in advance from a central computer, for airlines (such as Kenya Airways), hotels, long-distance buses and the SGR (Standard Gauge Railway) train. They are real-time, online systems: an agent or a customer on the internet checks availability, books, and pays, and the central database is updated immediately. To avoid double booking, the system locks a seat or room while one transaction is being processed, so no other terminal can book it at the same moment, and once sold it is shown as unavailable to everyone.

🎓 Education Card 4

Computer-aided instruction (CAI) uses the computer to teach: it presents lessons, drill-and-practice exercises and tests, and marks them, taking some of the teacher's role. Computer-aided learning (CAL) uses the computer as a resource that learners explore for themselves, such as encyclopaedias and tutorials. E-learning delivers lessons over the internet, so learners can study at a distance and at their own pace. Computer-based simulation models real situations, such as a science experiment or a flight, so learners can practise safely and cheaply.

📝 In the exam Card 5

KCSE often asks: "Explain two ways ICT is used in the banking industry", "Describe how a POS terminal helps in stock control", "Explain how a reservation system prevents double booking", or "Differentiate between CAI and CAL". Each point should name the system and say what it does, for example "ATMs allow customers to withdraw cash at any time without queueing in a banking hall". For communication, remember fax (sends a scanned copy of a document over telephone lines), email, radio and TV broadcasting, video conferencing (people in different places meet using cameras, microphones and screens over a network) and telecommuting (working from home using a computer linked to the office network).

K5 · ICT in Industry, Science & Society

Factories, research, transport, law and more · KCSE 451 syllabus · KCSE 451 · Form 4 · Application areas of ICT · lesson page

Reading cards

🏭 Industry Card 1

Industrial simulation models a system, such as a new production line or a nuclear plant, on a computer so designers can test it before building. Process control uses sensors to monitor a continuous process, such as temperature in a brewery or pressure in an oil refinery; the computer compares readings with set values and sends signals to actuators, devices such as motors, valves and switches that act on the process, to open a valve or switch a heater on or off automatically. Sensor in, computer decides, actuator acts: this loop repeats continuously (feedback). Computer-aided design (CAD) lets engineers and architects draw and test products and buildings on screen, and computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) uses those designs to control machines that cut and shape parts. Robots do repetitive, heavy or dangerous jobs, such as welding and painting car bodies, accurately and without tiring.

🏥 Robots in hospitals Card 2

In a hospital operating theatre, a surgeon can control a robotic arm from a console. Benefits: the robot makes very precise, steady movements with no hand tremor; it can work through small cuts, so patients lose less blood, have smaller scars and recover faster; it does not tire during long operations; and with a network link an expert surgeon can operate on or guide a patient far away (telesurgery). Robots also deliver medicines and supplies around hospitals and disinfect rooms, reducing staff exposure to infection. Drawbacks: they are very expensive to buy and maintain, staff need special training, and a fault during surgery can be dangerous.

🔬 Science, research and libraries Card 3

Weather forecasting uses powerful computers to process huge amounts of data from weather stations, satellites and radar, run models and predict the weather. In medical research computers analyse test results, model diseases and help design drugs; in hospitals expert systems help diagnose illness. The military uses ICT (Information and Communication Technology) for simulating battles, guiding missiles and keeping secure communication. Library systems record the lending of books (who borrowed what and when it is due), keep an inventory of stock, and hold a computerised catalogue so users can search by title, author or subject.

🚆 Transport, entertainment and the home Card 4

Air traffic control uses radar and computers to track aircraft and keep them safely apart during take-off, flight and landing. Shipping and railway control systems track vessels and trains and control signals and points. GPS (global positioning system) uses satellites to find the exact position of a vehicle, so companies can track lorries, buses and parcels. In entertainment, computers are used to play and create games, record and edit music, and edit video and make special effects. At home people use ICT for study, budgeting, communication and entertainment, and to control appliances.

🚗 Self-driving and self-parking vehicles Card 5

Computerised vehicles use sensors (cameras, radar and ultrasonic sensors), GPS and software to steer, brake and park without a human driver. Benefits to a town: fewer accidents caused by human error, such as speeding or drink-driving; vehicles park closer together and in tighter spaces, so less land is needed for parking; less time and fuel wasted circling to look for parking, which reduces congestion and pollution; and better mobility for elderly and disabled people. Concerns: they are expensive, software faults or hacking could cause crashes, it is unclear who is responsible after an accident, and jobs such as drivers and parking attendants may be lost.

🛡️ Expert systems, marketing, VR and law enforcement Card 6

An expert system copies the knowledge of a human expert. It has three parts: a knowledge base (facts and rules from experts), an inference engine (which applies the rules to the user's answers to reach a conclusion) and a user interface (through which the user asks and answers questions). In marketing, ICT supports e-commerce (buying and selling online), electronic presentations to clients, and advertising on websites and social media. Virtual reality (VR) creates a 3D artificial world that the user enters with a head-mounted display (HMD) and data gloves that sense hand movements. Law enforcement uses biometrics such as fingerprint matching to identify suspects, CCTV (Closed-Circuit Television) cameras to monitor public places, and computerised police records to store and search criminal records quickly.

KCSE often asks: "Explain three ways computers are used in industry", "Name the three components of an expert system and state the function of each", "Explain how ICT is used in weather forecasting", "State two devices used to interact with a virtual reality system", "Describe how computers are used in law enforcement", "State benefits of using robots in a hospital theatre" or "Explain benefits a town would gain from self-parking vehicles". Remember: process control uses sensors and works in real time; CAD is for designing, CAM is for manufacturing; an expert system is knowledge base + inference engine + user interface; VR uses a head-mounted display and data gloves. Always link the device or system to what it does.

K6 · Impact of ICT on Society

Jobs, health, environment, culture and the future · KCSE 451 syllabus · KCSE 451 · Form 4 · Impact of ICT on society · lesson page

Reading cards

💼 Effects on employment Card 1

ICT (Information and Communication Technology) affects employment in three ways. Job creation: new jobs appear, such as programmer, network administrator, web designer and computer technician. Job replacement: some jobs disappear and are taken over by people with ICT skills or by computerised machines, so workers who do not retrain may lose their jobs (for example, a manual filing clerk). Job displacement: a worker is moved to a different job or department, usually after retraining, instead of being laid off. Automated production, where computer-controlled machines and robots do repetitive work in factories, raises output and quality and lowers costs, but reduces the need for unskilled labour.

🩺 Workers' health and ergonomics Card 2

Long hours at a computer can cause repetitive strain injury (RSI), pain in the wrists, fingers and arms from repeated typing and mouse use; eye strain and headaches from glare and staring at the screen; back and neck problems from poor posture and badly designed furniture; and stress, including computer phobia (fear of using computers) and information overload. Older monitors were also said to give off low-level radiation. Ergonomics is the design of the working environment to suit the user: adjustable chairs with back support, the screen at eye level, anti-glare filters, wrist rests, good lighting and ventilation, and regular breaks.

🌍 Environment and culture Card 3

ICT uses a lot of electricity, and old computers, phones and batteries become e-waste that contains harmful substances such as lead and mercury; burning or dumping it pollutes soil, water and air, so it should be recycled through licensed e-waste handlers. On culture, ICT brings moral issues such as easy access to pornography and violent content, flaming (sending rude or abusive messages online), computer crime (hacking, fraud, piracy, cyberbullying) and erosion of culture, as young people adopt foreign values and languages from the internet. In Kenya, the Computer Misuse and Cybercrimes Act (2018) makes offences such as unauthorised access and cyber harassment punishable, and the Data Protection Act (2019) protects people's personal data.

🚀 Breakthroughs and future trends Card 4

ICT has brought breakthroughs in health care, such as expert systems that help diagnose illness and telemedicine, where a doctor treats or advises a patient far away through ICT, and in communication, through email, mobile phones, video calls and mobile money like M-Pesa. Computer systems keep evolving: hardware becomes smaller, faster and cheaper, and software more powerful and easier to use. Future trends centre on artificial intelligence (AI), which includes expert systems, natural language processing (computers understanding ordinary human language), voice recognition, robotics and artificial neural networks (systems that learn in a way modelled on the human brain). The internet, often called the information superhighway, links all of this together.

Natural language processing helps computers understand and respond to everyday human language, for example in chatbots and voice assistants.

Q8. What is an expert system?

  • A very fast supercomputer used by experts to process large amounts of data
  • A network that lets professionals such as doctors share files with each other
  • A trained technician who is an expert at repairing computer hardware

Expert systems are used, for example, to help doctors diagnose diseases or to advise farmers. They are a branch of artificial intelligence.

Q9. Which Kenyan law makes unauthorised access to computer systems and cyber harassment offences?

  • The Copyright Act
  • ✓ The Computer Misuse and Cybercrimes Act (2018)

Q10. What are artificial neural networks?

  • Networks of computers linking the departments of a hospital
  • Cables that connect a patient's brain directly to a computer
  • Computer networks that doctors use to share patients' brain scans between hospitals
  • ✓ Computer systems that learn from examples in a way modelled on the human brain

Neural networks are used for tasks like recognising faces, handwriting and speech. They are part of artificial intelligence.

K7 · Careers in ICT

Jobs, duties and where to study · KCSE 451 syllabus · KCSE 451 · Form 4 · Career opportunities in ICT · lesson page

Reading cards

🧑‍💻 Building systems and software Card 1

A computer operator enters data, operates and monitors computers and peripherals, keeps a log of activities and does basic troubleshooting. A programmer writes, tests, debugs, documents and maintains programs. A systems analyst studies the current system, identifies problems, recommends and designs a new system, and writes specifications for programmers. A chief programmer leads the programming team, assigns tasks and makes sure programs meet standards. A software engineer designs, develops and tests system and application software. A computer engineer designs, develops and maintains computer hardware and its components.

🌐 Data, networks and the web Card 2

A database administrator (DBA) designs and maintains the organisation's databases, controls who can access them, keeps data secure and accurate, and makes backups and restores data after a failure. A network administrator sets up and configures the network, monitors its performance, solves network problems and keeps it secure. A web administrator, or webmaster, develops, tests and updates the organisation's website, monitors traffic and responds to users' queries. A web designer plans and creates the layout and look of web pages. A graphic designer creates images, logos, adverts and other visual material.

🛠️ Support, management and business Card 3

A computer technician assembles, installs, repairs, upgrades and troubleshoots hardware and software. An ICT (Information and Communication Technology) trainer or teacher teaches ICT skills, prepares learning materials and assesses learners. An information systems manager plans and controls the ICT department and makes sure the organisation's information needs are met. A data processing manager supervises the data processing section and its staff so that data is processed accurately and on time. A computer sales representative sells hardware and software and advises customers on what to buy. An ICT entrepreneur runs an ICT business, such as a cyber café or a software firm, while an ICT consultant gives expert advice to organisations for a fee.

☁️ Cloud computing jobs Card 4

Cloud computing provides storage, software and computing power over the internet, as in Google Drive, Microsoft 365 or Amazon Web Services, instead of on the user's own computers. Cloud jobs include cloud engineer or architect (designs and builds cloud systems), cloud administrator (sets up and manages cloud services and user accounts) and cloud security specialist (protects data held in the cloud). Skills expected of a cloud worker: networking knowledge; data security, such as encryption and access control; operating systems, especially Linux; virtualisation (running many virtual computers on one physical server); database management; programming or scripting to automate tasks; and good problem-solving and communication skills to support customers.

🎓 Further education and choosing a course Card 5

After KCSE you can study ICT at public and private universities (degrees), national polytechnics and TVET (Technical and Vocational Education and Training) colleges (certificates and diplomas), or take professional certifications such as ICDL (International Certification of Digital Literacy), CCNA (Cisco Certified Network Associate) or vendor certificates from companies like Microsoft, Cisco and CompTIA. Government-sponsored places are allocated through KUCCPS (Kenya Universities and Colleges Central Placement Service). When choosing a course or institution, consider: accreditation (universities by the Commission for University Education, CUE, and TVET institutions by the TVET Authority, TVETA), entry requirements (your KCSE grade and subject cluster), cost of fees and upkeep, duration of the course, reputation of the institution, mode of study (full-time, part-time, evening or online), and your own interests and the job market.

📝 In the exam Card 6

KCSE typically asks: "State three duties of a database administrator", "Differentiate between a programmer and a systems analyst", "Name the ICT career responsible for maintaining a website" "State three factors to consider when choosing an institution for further training in ICT" or "State three skills expected of a person working for a cloud services company". Remember the key differences: the systems analyst designs the system, the programmer codes it, the computer operator runs it, the technician repairs it. A computer engineer works on hardware, a software engineer on software. Give duties as action phrases, such as "backing up the database", and give at least as many points as the question asks.
