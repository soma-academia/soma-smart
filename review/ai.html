A1 · What Is AI?

Machines that learn from examples · Going further · lesson page

Reading cards

🤖 Artificial intelligence Card 1

Artificial intelligence (AI) is a computer system that does tasks we normally think need human intelligence: recognising speech or faces, translating, predicting, or writing text. AI isn't magic, and it isn't alive. It is software that finds patterns in huge amounts of data.

📜 Rules or learning? Card 2

Ordinary programs follow rules a programmer wrote: "IF the mark is 50 or more, print Pass." Many AI systems instead learn their own rules from examples. Show a system thousands of emails marked "spam" or "not spam", and it learns what spam usually looks like, without anyone writing every rule.

📱 AI around you Card 3

You probably use AI every day: voice typing on your phone (including in Kiswahili), the spam folder in email, maps that predict traffic on Thika Road, apps that suggest the next video, face unlock, and translation apps. Banks and mobile-money services also use AI to flag unusual transactions that might be fraud.

🎯 Narrow AI Card 4

Today's AI is narrow: each system is good at one kind of task. A chess AI can't drive a car, and a translation app can't diagnose a sick goat. Films often show AI that thinks like a person about everything, but that doesn't exist.

⚠️ What AI can't do Card 5

AI can be confidently wrong. It doesn't understand the world the way you do, it can repeat unfairness hidden in its data, and it has no common sense outside its training. That's why people must check its work, especially for anything important like health, money or exams.

Narration

  • Artificial intelligence is software that finds patterns in lots of data.
  • Ordinary programs follow written rules; AI often learns rules from examples.
  • Voice typing, spam filters, maps and video suggestions all use AI.
  • Today's AI is narrow: each system does one kind of task.
  • AI can be confidently wrong, so people must check its work.
  • AI is a tool made by people, not a living thing.

A2 · Data & Patterns

Training data, labels and features · Going further · lesson page

Reading cards

📊 Data is AI's food Card 1

AI learns from data: photos, sounds, text, numbers. The more good, relevant examples it sees, the better it usually gets. A system that recognises maize leaf disease needs many photos of healthy and diseased leaves, taken in different light, on different phones.

🏷️ Labels Card 2

In supervised learning, every example comes with the right answer, called a label: this photo is "healthy", that one is "maize streak virus". The AI tries to find what separates the labels. Labelling is often done by people, and mistakes in labels become mistakes in the AI.

🔍 Features Card 3

Features are the details the AI looks at to make a decision. To tell mangoes from avocados, useful features might be colour, shape and skin texture. Choosing good features (or letting the AI discover them) is a big part of building a model.

🧪 Training data and test data Card 4

You don't test a learner with the exact questions they practised. In the same way, data is split: training data teaches the model, and separate test data, which the model has never seen, checks whether it really learned the pattern or just memorised the examples.

🗑️ Garbage in, garbage out Card 5

If the data is poor, so is the AI. Too few examples, wrong labels, or examples that only show one kind of situation (only sunny photos, only one region's accent) produce a model that fails in the real world. Good data is varied, correctly labelled and fairly collected.

Narration

  • AI learns from data: photos, sounds, text and numbers.
  • A label is the right answer attached to each example.
  • Features are the details the AI uses to decide.
  • Training data teaches the model; test data checks it.
  • Test data must be examples the model has never seen.
  • Garbage in, garbage out: poor data makes poor AI.

A3 · How Machines Learn

Models, decision trees and testing · Going further · lesson page

Reading cards

🧠 What a model is Card 1

When AI learns, it builds a model: a set of rules or numbers that turns inputs (features) into a prediction. A model for crop prices might take the season and the rainfall, and predict the price of maize next month. The model is the part that is "trained".

🌳 Decision trees Card 2

A decision tree is one simple kind of model. It asks yes/no questions, one after another, until it reaches an answer: "Does it have feathers? Yes → bird. No → Does it breathe with gills? Yes → fish. No → mammal." Training means finding which questions to ask, and in what order.

🔁 Training is trial and improvement Card 3

Most models start bad and improve. They make predictions on the training data, measure how wrong they are, adjust a little, and repeat, often thousands or millions of times. Neural networks, the models behind modern speech and image AI, learn this way by adjusting millions of numbers.

📈 Accuracy and testing Card 4

Accuracy is the share of predictions that are correct. A model that is right 90 times out of 100 has 90% accuracy. It must be measured on test data the model has never seen. A model can score 100% on its training data and still fail on new examples.

🎯 Overfitting Card 5

Overfitting is when a model memorises its training examples instead of learning the general pattern, like a learner who memorises past-paper answers but can't solve a new question. In the explainer above, a tree can sort every training animal perfectly and still get a dolphin wrong.

Narration

  • A trained model turns inputs into a prediction.
  • A decision tree asks yes or no questions until it reaches an answer.
  • Training is trial and improvement, repeated many times.
  • Neural networks learn by adjusting millions of numbers.
  • Accuracy is the share of predictions that are correct.
  • Always measure accuracy on test data the model hasn't seen.
  • Overfitting is memorising the examples instead of the pattern.

A4 · Chatbots & Generative AI

How AI writes text and makes images · Going further · lesson page

Reading cards

✍️ Generative AI Card 1

Generative AI creates new content: text, images, music, or computer code. AI chatbots write answers, and image generators turn a short description into a picture. They work by learning patterns from enormous amounts of existing text and images.

🔮 Predicting the next word Card 2

A chatbot is built on a large language model. It was trained on billions of sentences to predict what word, or piece of a word, comes next. To answer you, it predicts one piece, adds it, and predicts again, over and over. The explainer above does the same thing with just a few sentences.

🤥 Confident but wrong Card 3

Because a chatbot predicts likely-sounding words rather than looking up facts, it can make things up, such as fake dates, quotes or sources, and say them confidently. This is often called "hallucination". Always check important facts against a reliable source, like your textbook, a teacher or an official website.

🌍 What it knows depends on its data Card 4

A model only knows patterns from its training data, which stops at a certain date. Languages with less text online, like Kiswahili, Sheng and other Kenyan languages, usually get weaker answers than English. Missing or one-sided data shows up in the answers.

💬 Good prompts, good use Card 5

A prompt is what you type to the chatbot. Clear prompts get better answers: say who you are, what you need, and how you want it ("Explain photosynthesis to a Grade 7 learner in 5 short points"). Use it to explain, quiz you, or give feedback, not to do your work for you.

Narration

  • Generative AI creates new text, images, music or code.
  • A chatbot predicts the next word again and again.
  • It can make up facts and sound very sure about them.
  • Always check important facts against a reliable source.
  • It knows only what was in its training data, which ends at a certain date.
  • Kiswahili and Sheng answers are often weaker, because there was less training text.
  • Clear prompts get better answers.

A5 · Bias & Fairness

When AI treats people unfairly · Going further · lesson page

Reading cards

⚖️ What AI bias is Card 1

AI bias is when a system treats some groups of people unfairly, for example making more mistakes for women, for darker skin, for older people, or for one region's accent. AI doesn't choose to be unfair: it copies patterns, including unfair ones, from the data it learned from.

🔬 A real example: faces Card 2

In 2018, researchers Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru tested commercial face-analysis systems. The systems were far less accurate for darker-skinned women than for lighter-skinned men. The training data had mostly lighter-skinned faces, so the systems learned them best.

📄 Another example: hiring Card 3

A large company built an AI to sort job applications, trained on CVs from people it had hired in the past, who were mostly men. The tool learned to rank women's CVs lower, and the company stopped using it. The AI faithfully copied an unfair history.

🗣️ Bias close to home Card 4

Voice assistants may understand some accents better than others. A credit-scoring app could learn to give fewer loans to people from certain areas if its data reflects past unfairness. In Kenya, where people speak many languages and live very different lives, data from one group may not represent everyone.

🛠️ Making AI fairer Card 5

Fairer AI needs varied data that represents everyone it will affect, testing accuracy separately for different groups, people reviewing important decisions, and openness about how a system decides. Asking "who could this hurt, and who is missing from the data?" is a skill everyone can practise.

Narration

  • AI bias is when a system treats some groups unfairly.
  • AI copies patterns, including unfair ones, from its training data.
  • Face systems were once far less accurate for darker-skinned women.
  • A hiring AI learned to prefer men because its past data did.
  • Bias can affect accents, loans and much more.
  • Fairer AI needs varied data, testing for every group, and human review.
  • Always ask: who could this hurt, and who is missing from the data?

A6 · Using AI Safely & Honestly

Deepfakes, privacy and honest work · Going further · lesson page

Reading cards

🎭 Deepfakes Card 1

AI can now make fake videos, photos and voices that look and sound real; these are called deepfakes. They can show a leader saying something they never said, or put someone's face in a photo they were never in. Be extra careful with shocking clips, especially around elections.

📞 Voice-clone scams Card 2

Scammers can copy a voice from a short recording. A caller who sounds exactly like a relative may say they're in trouble and need M-Pesa urgently. Hang up and call the person back on the number you know, or ask a question only they could answer. Never send money because of a voice alone.

🔍 Check before you share Card 3

Before believing or forwarding something: Who posted it first? Do trusted news sources report it? Does the image have odd details, like strange hands, blurry text or mismatched shadows? If you can't confirm it, don't share it. Spreading fakes can hurt real people.

🔒 Protect your privacy Card 4

Anything you type or upload into an AI app may be stored by the company. Never share passwords, PINs, your ID number, your address, or photos of other people without permission. Kenya's Data Protection Act (2019) gives you rights over your personal data, but the safest data is the data you never share.

🧭 Honest, healthy use Card 5

Use AI to help you learn, not to pretend its work is yours. Follow your school's rules, and say when you used AI. Remember that running AI uses a lot of electricity and water in data centres, so use it when it really helps. And AI is a tool: for worries and feelings, talk to people you trust.
